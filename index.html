What does a good solution need to achieve?

Three questions

Baseline

What are today's surface-water and groundwater conditions?

2
Impact

Who or what is affected—buildings, roads, ecosystems, soil, and neighbouring sites?

3
Mitigation

How will the development avoid, reduce, or manage its impacts?

Practical aims: manage peak discharge, reduce runoff volumes and pollutant loads where feasible, and protect downstream receptors.
Current policy context · England

SuDS belongs in the development design

The September 2026 NPPF Chapter 18, policy F8, addresses sustainable drainage, flow rates, runoff volumes, and wider benefits. The 2025 National SuDS Standards provide the design framework.

These are planning principles, not a substitute for site-specific planning, engineering, and regulatory assessment.

Policy context updated October 2026.
Impacts and receptors

Environmental impact assessment considers baseline conditions, the pressures introduced by development, and ways to mitigate harm. A drainage scheme should account for flood risk, water quality, biodiversity, downstream receptors, and maintenance.

03 / Understand the objective

Runoff is a question of volume and timing.

Read the hydrograph

A development can increase the runoff peak and alter the timing and duration of discharge. Sustainable drainage aims to change those dynamics.

Peak flow: How high is the maximum rate?
Volume: How much runoff is discharged in total?
Timing: When does water reach downstream receptors?
Ask: can two designs have the same runoff volume but very different downstream impacts?
Volume and peak discharge

The area beneath a discharge hydrograph represents water volume. The maximum flow is its peak discharge. Storage may reduce and delay a peak while having less effect on the total volume unless water also infiltrates, evaporates, or is reused.

07 / From observation to design

What does the model reveal?

Engineering interpretation: High ground may drain rapidly while transferring water towards more exposed downstream locations. Flat sections may require a different conveyance and storage approach.
What models can reveal

Hydrodynamic models can indicate possible flow pathways and flood accumulation under specified scenarios. Interpretation still depends on rainfall inputs, terrain, drainage representation, boundary conditions, and model uncertainty.

08 / Options for surface water

Where can the water go?

↓
Infiltrate

Groundwater recharge if soils, groundwater, contamination, and adjacent structures allow it.

▤
Store

Attenuation basins, ponds, swales, tanks, and other storage infrastructure.

→
Convey

Move water through suitable channels, pipes, or landscape features to appropriate destinations.

↻
Reuse

Where appropriate, capture rainwater for non-potable uses such as irrigation or toilet flushing.

▶Watch the permeable-paving demonstration: Permeable Paving vs 3 Tonnes of Water ↗
Drainage pathways

Infiltration, attenuation, conveyance, and reuse address different parts of stormwater management. Most practical schemes combine interventions, taking account of site constraints and intended performance.

Three questions before proposing infiltration

1
Geology and strata

Which formations and superficial deposits occur nearby?

2
Borehole evidence

What does the available ground investigation suggest, and how representative is it?

3
Constraints

Soils, contamination, water table, stability, and neighbouring assets

Geology and infiltration

Published geology maps and borehole logs help to form an initial ground model. Suitable infiltration design also requires site-specific evidence, including permeability, groundwater, contamination, and geotechnical constraints.

10 / Benefits and constraints

Water is a resource—and carries pollutants.

Rainwater harvesting, detention, and conveyance can all be designed for wider environmental benefits. Water quality depends on source, land use, and intended use.

Ask before proposing reuse

Source: roof runoff, hardstanding runoff, or household greywater?
Quality: what pollutants and treatment requirements are relevant?
Demand: will there be reliable non-potable demand when water is available?
Operation: who inspects, maintains, and operates the system?
Rainwater harvesting and greywater recycling are related but distinct systems: their sources, treatment, and storage differ.
Water quality

Rainwater, surface runoff, and domestic greywater are different sources with different quality concerns. Reuse options depend on treatment, exposure risk, applicable requirements, and the timing of supply and demand.

13 / Turning rainfall into a design input

The shape of a storm matters.

Original 5-minute-labelled rainfall depth sequence

The example design-storm dataset reports approximately 49.03 mm over one hour. The plotted ordinates are reproduced as provided.

For the next conceptual calculation, we use a rounded 50 mm storm depth. The engineering lesson is the relationship between depth, area, generated runoff, and storage.
Design storm shape

A design storm describes rainfall through time. Two events with the same total rainfall depth may produce different peak flows because of the temporal distribution, drainage response, and antecedent conditions.

Locating interventions

A useful concept considers low points, topography, available space, gravity drainage, existing utilities, exceedance routes, access, public safety, and maintenance. A combination of smaller interventions may be preferable to one large structure.

16 / Water reuse

What does 6,000 m³ mean in practice?

Scale check

Around one million flushes

6,000 m³ of water ÷ 0.006 m³ per flush = 1,000,000 flush equivalents.

Could we really reuse all that water?

Consider tank size, rainfall and demand timing, water quality, treatment, and long-term operation.

Conceptual scale comparison for rainwater reuse (not household greywater).
A 'flush equivalent' illustrates scale only. It is not a prediction of usable rainwater or a specification for a rainwater-harvesting system.
Reuse versus storm storage

The one-million-flush comparison expresses the magnitude of 6,000 m³: at 6 L per flush that is about one million equivalent flushes. Actual reuse requires suitable water quality, storage, demand, and operation over time.

18 / Takeaways

Key takeaways

1
Understand site hydrology

Study contours, gradients, soils, receiving waters, and infrastructure.

2
Quantify rainfall and runoff

Estimate volumes and identify the assumptions behind the calculations.

3
Design a sustainable water strategy

Combine source control, infiltration where suitable, storage, conveyance, and reuse.

4
Assess risk over time

Protect environmental receptors, consider climate change and maintenance, and seek additional evidence.

Final question
What one additional piece of information would most improve your drainage design?

Examples: infiltration test, allowable discharge rate, high-resolution site levels, drainage connections, groundwater data, or downstream constraints.

Essential takeaways

Understand the site, quantify runoff, select a suitable mix of SuDS measures, assess downstream effects, and plan for future conditions. Initial calculations are screening tools that inform more detailed design.
